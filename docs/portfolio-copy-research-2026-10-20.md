# Portfolio copy: ten primary-source studies

Research date: 20 October 2026. Scope: the language across Sachit's site, with project descriptions as the priority. This is a research and proposed-copy document, not a claim that the site-wide rewrite has been published.

## Method and limits

Read the authors' own pages and substantive project/build narratives, not a roundup of portfolio thumbnails. The sample includes short project notes, conventional case studies, a personal build article and a work reflection; these are identified below rather than pretending all ten have the same format. Search discovery was followed by direct page reading. Agent Reach's Exa command was unavailable, so the available web search/page reader was used instead.

Simon Pan's Uber page returned an access error; Robb Owen's page was blocked and Sean Halpin's returned no readable text. None counts toward the ten. Numbers below are claims made by their authors, not independently audited business results. Short quotations are attributed; proposed copy does not borrow their claims.

## 1. Brittany Chiang — short project descriptions

Sources: [portfolio and projects](https://brittanychiang.com/).

Example: “Web app for visualizing personalized Spotify data.”

The Spotify Profile entry defines the object before describing what someone can do: inspect listening data and create playlists. Technologies sit in separate tags. Halcyon's install count and the previous portfolio's stars/forks attach to identifiable public artifacts, rather than floating beside the author's name as general credibility.

Grammar: present tense for a working project's behavior; compact noun phrases in the index; readable verbs rather than a chain of adjectives. Her broader biography is more promotional than the project entries—we should not copy every part of it.

Adopt: a literal first sentence and a small number of concrete functions. Adapt: technology tags, but avoid repeating the same stack in the description. Reject: importing her popularity metrics or generic personal-brand phrases.

## 2. Tania Rascia — TakeNote

Sources: [project index](https://www.taniarascia.com/projects/), [Building TakeNote](https://www.taniarascia.com/building-takenote/).

Example heading: “Why I Don't Want to Ship It”.

The index calls it an open-source notes app and links to the demo, article and source. The article explains the personal frustration, requirements, storage/authentication choices and difficult GitHub synchronization. It distinguishes the static demo from the authenticated self-hosted version, credits contributors and explains why maintaining a hosted product was not worth the privacy and operational burden.

Grammar: first-person past tense for decisions, present tense for app behavior, contractions and direct admissions. Sections answer real development questions rather than mechanically following Challenge / Solution / Impact.

Adopt: separate demo, development and live status; explain one genuinely difficult implementation detail; credit other people's work. Reject: making every project sound like a frictionless launch. QT and Thekuwa particularly need this status honesty.

## 3. Ian Lunn — LSU Football Operations

Sources: [portfolio](https://ianlunn.co.uk/), [LSU project](https://ianlunn.co.uk/portfolio/lsu-football-ops/).

Example: “Red Six Media provided me with desktop layouts”.

He establishes the client relationship and his front-end responsibility, rather than claiming the supplied design. The narrative connects requirements to choices: image-heavy pages, device performance, restricted mobile animation, browser capability checks, social crawler behavior and a documented handover. Technologies are explained where they solve something.

Grammar: active first-person verbs, explicit collaborator names, past tense for the engagement. A named testimonial is distinct from the implementation account. Company achievements elsewhere on the portfolio are context, not automatically caused by his code.

Adopt: define what was supplied and what Sachit built; describe the constraint behind a technical decision. Reject: treating a client's growth or funding as the developer's personal outcome.

## 4. Adham Dannaway — lean design system

Sources: [portfolio](https://www.adhamdannaway.com/portfolio), [Creating a lean design system](https://www.adhamdannaway.com/portfolio/creating-a-lean-design-system).

Example: “My goal was to keep things simple and flexible”.

The case names the organization, fragmented products and limited team capacity. It then shows the actual work: screenshot audit, component consolidation, mapping old to new, shared styles, libraries, workshops and handover. Importantly, the intended coded system did not reach implementation before the acquisition; the article says so.

Grammar: I for his actions, we for shared decisions; clear sentence-case headings; concrete artifacts rather than adjectives about quality. His own voice guidance favors short sentences, strong verbs and consistent vocabulary.

Adopt: delivered work and planned work must be separate. A screenshot or prototype cannot establish a shipped backend. Reject: describing every design intention as a completed production feature.

## 5. Frank Chimero — Aaarrrppp

Source: [project note](https://frankchimero.com/projects/arp/).

Example: “a work-in-progress browser-based instrument that translates images into music”.

This short project note explains the input and output, then the mechanism: image properties influence musical properties, with an accompanying visual strip. It links the instrument and credits the example imagery. It does not manufacture a business result for an experimental tool.

Grammar: present tense, specific nouns, a brief descriptor followed by explanatory sentences. The work-in-progress qualification is part of the definition, not buried in a disclaimer.

Adopt: describing useful behavior can be enough; not every project needs a KPI. Reject: calling an ordinary tool innovative, seamless or transformative to compensate for missing evidence.

## 6. Tobias Ahlin — TypeSource and smaller tools

Sources: [portfolio project notes](https://tobiasahlin.com/), [TypeSource](https://tobiasahlin.com/typesource/).

Example: “I created TypeSource as a way to browse web fonts”.

The project teaser explains the motivation—browsing by appearance rather than names and attributes—and the linked project lets the reader use it. SpinKit and Moving Letters similarly identify their actual output. His larger professional roles are described separately from these independent tools. TypeSource itself is a specimen/tool, not a long case study.

Grammar: a first-person creation statement followed by the reader's task. Short notes do not all need an introductory problem paragraph.

Adopt: direct project links and one clear reason the tool exists. Reject: forcing every entry into an identical five-paragraph narrative.

## 7. Josh W. Comeau — rebuilding his blog

Source: [How I Built My Blog](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/blog/how-i-built-my-blog-v2/). Format: personal project build article, not a conventional portfolio case study. Read the relevant stack, styling, syntax-highlighting, collaboration and transition sections.

Example: “There are a few reasons”.

He explains why he chose tools, not merely which tools he knows. Concrete comparisons include styling output and syntax-highlighting costs, with caveats about what is being measured. Alternatives, migration pain and interaction shortcomings remain visible. The technical detail earns its length because it answers implementation questions.

Grammar: contractions, varied sentence lengths, first-person reasoning and plain admissions. The structure follows decisions, not a universal case-study template.

Adopt: one defensible technical choice and tradeoff per substantial project. Reject: copying the article's length or turning every card into a tutorial. Its framework details are historical context, not recommendations for this codebase.

## 8. Maggie Appleton — leaving Elicit

Source: [Leaving Elicit](https://maggieappleton.com/leaving-elicit). Format: work reflection, not a conventional case study.

Example: “with assistance from colleagues”.

She describes being the first designer, the tasks she covered and the constraints of the role. Company growth figures are presented as company growth, while her responsibilities are separately explained. Team assistance and personal limitations are explicit rather than erased to make a heroic solo narrative.

Grammar: I for experience and responsibility; we for collective results. Specific working circumstances make the voice personal without needing quirky wording in every sentence.

Adopt: separate the organization's numbers from Sachit's contribution; distinguish sole-developer responsibility from sole authorship of every input. Reject: borrowing organizational growth as individual impact or adding personal drama where it does not help the reader understand the work.

## 9. Sander de Snaijer — portfolio and SEO account

Sources: [project entry](https://www.sanderdesnaijer.com/projects/portfolio), [build account](https://www.sanderdesnaijer.com/blog/nextjs-sanity-portfolio-headless-cms), [SEO account](https://www.sanderdesnaijer.com/blog/from-zero-to-27k-impressions-seo).

Example: “I don't have a definitive answer for what caused this one.”

The project entry's broad tooling language is less useful than the detailed articles: schemas, test areas, RSS limits and discarded motion. The SEO account gives a reporting window, impressions, clicks and concentration in one article. It does not pretend to know the cause of every movement in the data.

Grammar: concrete implementation nouns and qualified causal statements work better than the generic project introduction. This is a mixed reference, not a model to copy wholesale.

Adopt: metrics need a source, period, denominator where relevant and a clear relationship to the work. Reject: a large isolated number beside a project title or claiming correlation as causation.

## 10. Tan Pham — school website and Getahead

Sources: [projects](https://www.tanpham.dev/projects), [Japan International School](https://www.tanpham.dev/projects/japan-international-school/), [Getahead](https://www.tanpham.dev/projects/australia-job-platform-startup/).

Examples: “on tested public pages at delivery”; “company-level traction after launch”.

The school case identifies a bilingual site, content system, deployment resources, integration and maintenance responsibilities. Its Lighthouse claim is bounded to tested pages at delivery. Getahead identifies the specific front-end flows and distinguishes the engineer's contribution from the company's funding and sign-up figures.

Grammar: action-first scope statements, precise qualifiers and concrete system boundaries. Question headings help scanning, but using that same pattern on every Sachit project would become repetitive.

Adopt: this is the closest model for separating full-stack scope, operational constraints and externally reported company context. Reject: attributing a whole product or company result to a single interface implementation.

## What the ten actually suggest

1. Start with the thing: a company website, staff dashboard or contract tool. Avoid vague substitutes such as digital experience and platform when they conceal the function.
2. Put Sachit's contribution next to the screenshot. I built should name a component, flow, API, schema or deployment—not claim the whole client's success.
3. Present tense describes behavior; past tense describes work performed. Use I for individual actions and we only when a team is actually involved.
4. A card needs one or two useful sentences. Detail pages can explain a difficult decision, constraints and outcome. Do not repeat the same description across Hero, rail, Approach and Services.
5. Mix sentence lengths naturally. Do not replace every sentence with a slogan, em dash or three-part list. Nor should every case have the same Challenge / Solution / Impact formula.
6. Metrics are optional. Demo values are not impact. Client capacity, age or funding is context, not Sachit's achievement. Avoid causality without evidence.
7. Links, status, dates and role are data, not decoration. Keep them explicit and consistent. A public demo is not proof of a production deployment or a secure backend.

## Current-site findings, in priority order

### Claims need correction before tone polish

- `src/app/layout.tsx` says AI automation is wired into live products. `PRODUCT.md` explicitly says shipped AI-automation evidence is missing. The current metadata overstates the available evidence.
- Retail HR's employee and attendance values are dashboard/demo values, not demonstrated production adoption or improved attendance. Do not publish them as personal impact. The source and displayed screenshot also disagree on the employee count.
- Unitech's MW figures describe client assets; Lama Group's 20+ years describes the client's age. If retained, label them as project/client context, never personal results.
- Lama Fuel's copy claims ordering, dispatch visibility and replacing phone/fax coordination. The supplied website screenshot does not establish these workflows or that prior process. Confirm actual implemented scope before retaining this story.
- PRODUCT's evidence inventory predates the current six-project selection and demo-status decisions. Do not treat its older employee count or five-live-systems total as newly verified facts.

### Wording that weakens the voice

- “the repeated manual work automated away” is an awkward passive fragment. Name the actual task.
- “single sign-on holding it up” and “when that's what ships the work” obscure a simple explanation behind metaphors.
- “Not a menu of packages” discusses the writing instead of helping a recruiter understand the work.
- “Real content structures, not a static brochure” is a stock contrast. Specify the content types or editing workflow if confirmed.
- “without waiting on a separate designer” claims an unmeasured team benefit and unnecessarily dismisses collaboration. State the design background instead.
- “warm, image-led ... one brand story” describes art direction more than the site's function. A single phrase may fit, but the whole card should not speak like a design-agency pitch.
- Repeating first screen / live product / systems behind it across sections makes the site feel generated from one reusable prompt.

These are observable writing problems, not proof of AI authorship. Keep useful existing phrases and the approved visual composition; do not flatten everything into identical corporate prose.

Use a separate role line, status and live-site link. Keep the card description short; explain schema, authentication and difficult decisions on detail pages after checking the facts.

### Approach paragraph

Draft: I design interfaces and build the APIs and databases behind them. My work includes company websites, staff dashboards and contract tools.

This removes unsupported autonomy without changing the protected Hero composition. Workflow automation can have its own concrete sentence once a real approval or scheduled-job example is documented. The approved Hero headline remains untouched in this research pass.

### Bio / rail

Draft: I'm Sachit, a developer based in Kathmandu. I spent two years in design before moving into development, then worked as the sole developer at Lama Group for a year.

The durations come from the project brief, not newly checked employment records. Confirm chronology/current employment before publishing this exact sentence.

### Services

Prefer a plain heading such as What I work on. Remove the preamble about menus and proof. Keep concrete categories, then describe actual scope:

- Web applications: Staff dashboards and internal tools, from the interface to the API and database.
- Backend and APIs: PostgreSQL schemas, Node and Express services, authentication and JWT-based single sign-on.
- Company websites: Responsive pages for company information, projects and enquiries.
- Interface design: Layouts and interaction design in Figma, followed through into the working interface.

These are proposed wording, not a new package offering. Remove booking systems unless there is a real example. Do not call APIs secure without a specific, tested security claim.

### Metadata

Draft description: Sachit Ghimire is a Kathmandu-based developer with a design background. Selected websites and internal tools built with Next.js, Node.js and PostgreSQL.

Avoid live AI product claims until a shipped example supports them. Availability for AI-automation roles can remain in Contact; interest and demonstrated delivery are different facts.
